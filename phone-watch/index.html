Talk about it

  1. Does the number in your city surprise you? Why might it be higher or lower than you guessed?
  2. The computer only counts phones it can see. Who is it missing, and does that push the number up or down?
  3. Is "on a phone" the same as "addicted"? What would you need to see to tell the difference?
  4. Count your own street for five minutes. How close do you get to the computer?
How this works, and where it goes wrong

A small program grabs one frame from each camera every hour. The cameras are live streams their owners share publicly on YouTube: Shibuya Center Gai in Tokyo (Shibuya Television), Dotonbori in Osaka (RVJJP), Abbey Road crossing in London (EarthCam), Bryant Park in New York (Bryant Park NYC), Hongdae crossing in Seoul (Hongdae Live view), Sukhumvit Soi 11 in Bangkok (The Real Samui Webcam), Agdao street corner in Davao City (JazBaz Philippines), city-centre intersection in Melbourne (Cityscape Live), Temple Bar in Dublin (EarthCam) and Copacabana promenade in Rio de Janeiro (Homes in Rio).

A vision model running on one computer, Qwen3-VL 30B-A3B, draws a box around each person close enough to judge. Then it zooms in on each person, one at a time, and notes whether it can see a phone, where their hands are, and whether they are looking down at them. A phone in hand, or both hands together in front with eyes on them, counts as on a phone. Unclear people are left out of the share. Nothing leaves that computer except the counts on this page: the latest frame from each camera is shown above, and every other photo is deleted after three days.

It makes mistakes. Umbrellas, coats, night, rain on the lens and people facing away all hide phones, so the real numbers are probably higher. The cameras sit at different heights, so the New York camera sees fewer people up close than the Tokyo one. Treat each number as a rough count, not a fact about a whole city.
